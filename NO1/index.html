0:35–1:25 · The network

Imagine a ring that sees a pulse at the finger, a watch that sees the pulse and motion at the wrist, and an ear device that adds another location. Research hearables have demonstrated cardiac and respiratory sensing. That does not mean every AirPod or earbud exposes these signals. This is a future network of compatible sensors. If each device gives me an average heart rate, I can compare summaries. If they give me raw waveforms on the same clock, I can study how those signals relate in time. 1:25–2:30 · The physics

Every heartbeat launches a pressure wave. Optical sensors see the resulting blood-volume pulse; that technique is photoplethysmography, or PPG. At two sites on the same arterial route, the pulse arrives at different times. The difference is pulse transit time, or PTT. Divide the arterial path length by that time and you get pulse-wave velocity. Here is an illustrative segment: twenty centimetres and forty milliseconds gives five metres per second. These are teaching numbers, not measured SensWear data or a healthy-range threshold. We compare the start of the pulse upstroke, because peaks can shift as waveforms change. This is pressure-wave speed, not blood-cell speed.

Sources: Mehrgardt et al., Pulse Transit Time PPG Dataset, https://physionet.org/content/pulse-transit-time-ppg/1.1.0/ . Waveforms in this talk are synthetic. Ring and wrist would require a suitable arterial route and path estimate.

3:35–4:35 · Demo 2

A more compliant vessel expands as the wave passes. A stiffer vessel carries the disturbance faster. [Increase relative stiffness and replay.] The travelling glow represents a pressure disturbance, not flowing blood. We slow the motion down so you can see it. In this simple tube model, quadrupling effective wall stiffness doubles the wave speed. Real biology is more complicated. Pressure, muscle tone, wall thickness and diameter all matter. So we can talk about stiffness-related trends, but PWV alone does not directly measure intrinsic arterial elasticity. And a wrist–finger segment does not measure central aortic stiffness.

4:35–5:45 · The health opportunities

Pulse velocity is one example. A network could track vascular dynamics under comparable conditions. Multiple sites also let us ask whether circulation changes are regional or more widespread. A pilot study found differences in bilateral pulse variability in people with peripheral arterial disease; it did not validate a ring-and-watch diagnostic. Another benefit is confidence: a noisy wrist signal coinciding with movement may be less trustworthy than a clean signal elsewhere. Finally, shared time lets us relate pulse dynamics to breathing, posture and activity. Not every question needs millisecond precision. Pulse transit across a short segment does; long-term temperature trends usually do not.

Sources: multi-site perfusion pilot, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6165367/ ; synchronized PPG/ECG/inertial dataset, https://physionet.org/content/pulse-transit-time-ppg/1.1.0/ ; research hearable, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-06925-2 . Recovery framing is a research proposal, not a proven outcome.

5:45–6:45 · What synchronization means

The timestamp should describe acquisition at the sensor. A packet can sit in a buffer, travel over Bluetooth, and wait in a phone queue. Its arrival time includes all of those delays. We need a mapping from each local clock to a common timebase, correcting both offset and clock rate. We must check it again as the clocks drift. Even a perfect clock does not solve sampling or filtering. At a hundred hertz, samples are ten milliseconds apart. Interpolation can help estimate an upstroke, but it cannot manufacture missing bandwidth or undo poor signal quality. The useful engineering metric is measured end-to-end timing uncertainty.

6:45–7:55 · SensWear

This is why we are building SensWear. The same flexible main board can work with different sensor modules and wearable forms. You can access raw signals and timestamps, inspect the hardware, and change the firmware. That gives researchers the ingredients to build a network and test the assumptions themselves. Precise multi-device synchronization is the experiment I am proposing here, not a performance claim about the current platform. The public clock-setting interface uses whole seconds; it is not proof of millisecond alignment. To make pulse transit measurements credible, we would have to implement and measure the full acquisition-time alignment.

7:55–9:00 · A concrete next step

I would start with two compatible PPG nodes, at the wrist and finger, on the same side. First test timing against a common input and reference clock. Measure the residual offset, jitter and drift over the intended recording duration. Then verify the real sampling and processing delays, the signal quality and the arterial path estimate. Finally compare the derived transit measurements with a suitable reference under controlled conditions and report the failures. The question is whether a physiological change is larger than our uncertainty. One further distinction: ECG to finger timing includes the heart’s pre-ejection period, so it is pulse arrival time, not pure vascular transit time.

Source for PAT=PEP+PTT: Zhang et al., Journal of Applied Physiology (2011), https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00980.2011 . 9:00–10:00 · Close and invitation

The idea I want to leave you with is that our bodies generate connected events, while our devices often report disconnected summaries.
